Gauge converter

Your swatch came out different from the pattern? See how big your piece will turn out, and how many stitches and rows to use instead.

Quick answer: finished width = stitches ÷ your stitches per inch. A pattern with 100 stitches at 20 sts per 4 in (5 per inch) comes out 20 in wide. If your swatch has 18 sts per 4 in (4.5 per inch), the same 100 stitches make 22.2 in. More stitches than the pattern: go up a needle or hook size. Fewer: go down a size. Should I change needles or the numbers?

If you have more stitches than the pattern, your fabric is tighter: go up a needle or hook size. If you have fewer, it's looser: go down a size. Changing tool size is usually the best first fix, because it keeps the fabric feel the designer intended.

If you love your fabric as it is, use the new stitch and row numbers above instead. Round to suit your stitch pattern: if it repeats over 4 stitches, pick the nearest multiple of 4.

Measure your swatch after washing and drying it the way you'll treat the finished piece. Many yarns grow or shrink a little.

Plain maths: stitches ÷ (your stitches over 4 in ÷ 4). The thicker the yarn, the more one stitch matters.

What does "20 sts and 28 rows = 4 in" mean?

It's the pattern's gauge (tension in the UK): in a square of the fabric 4 in (10 cm) across, there are 20 stitches side to side and 28 rows top to bottom, with the needle or hook size given and usually after blocking. It tells you the size the designer's numbers will make.

Does row gauge matter?

Less than stitch gauge, for most patterns, because they usually say "work until the piece measures 10 in" rather than give a row count. It does matter when the pattern counts rows (raglan sleeves, top-down yokes, colourwork charts, crochet worked to a set number of rows) and for how much yarn you use. If only your rows are off, keep your needle and work to the length instead of the row count.

How do I measure a swatch?

  1. Make a swatch at least 6 inches (15 cm) wide in the stitch the pattern uses for its gauge, with the same needles or hook.
  2. Wash and dry it, then lay it flat without stretching.
  3. Count the stitches across 4 inches (10 cm) in the middle, away from the edges. Count half stitches too.
  4. Do the same for rows, measuring up and down.

For hats, socks and anything else worked in the round, knit the swatch in the round too if you can. Many knitters purl a little looser than they knit, so a flat swatch can give a slightly different number.

Questions crafters ask

How do I measure gauge in crochet?

Crochet a swatch at least 6 inches wide in the pattern's stitch, block it, then count the stitches and rows over 4 inches (10 cm) in the middle. Include half stitches.

What is gauge in knitting and crochet?

Gauge (called tension in the UK) is how many stitches and rows fit in a set width, usually 4 inches or 10 cm. Matching it is what makes a garment come out the size the pattern says.

Does gauge matter for scarves and blankets?

Less. A scarf a little wider is usually fine, but it changes how much yarn you need. For fitted garments, hats and socks it matters a lot.

Why is my gauge different from the pattern?

Everyone holds yarn with a different tension, and needle materials and yarn brands vary. That's normal, which is why patterns ask you to swatch.

How do I work out the finished size from my gauge?

Divide the number of stitches by your stitches per inch. 100 stitches at 4.5 stitches per inch make a piece about 22.2 in wide.

If I have too many stitches, do I go up or down a needle size?

Up. More stitches per 4 in means your fabric is tighter than the pattern's, so a bigger needle or hook makes bigger stitches. Too few stitches: go down a size.

Does row gauge matter?

Less than stitch gauge for most patterns, which say to work to a length. It matters when a pattern counts rows, such as raglans, yokes, charts and crochet worked to a set number of rows.
