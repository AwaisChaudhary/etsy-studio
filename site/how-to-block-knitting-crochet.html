How to block knitting and crochet

Blocking is the last step that makes a finished piece look finished: stitches even out, edges straighten and lace opens up. Here are wet, steam and spray blocking with our own drawings, and a helper that picks the method for your yarn and works out the mats and pins you need.

Quick answer: to block knitting or crochet, wet it, shape it and let it dry. For wool and most natural fibres, soak the piece in lukewarm water with a little wool wash for 15 to 30 minutes, press the water out in a towel (don't wring), lay it flat on blocking mats or towels, pin it to the measurements and leave it until completely dry, usually a day or two. Acrylic doesn't hold a new shape from water, so pin it dry and steam block it: hold a steam iron just above it, never touching. For a light tidy-up, pin it out and spray it with water.

Blocking methods at a glance

What is blocking, and do I need to do it?

Blocking means getting a finished piece wet (or steamed), shaping it and letting it dry in that shape. It evens out wobbly stitches, flattens curling edges, makes pieces the right size to sew together and opens up lace so the pattern shows. Lace and colourwork really need it. Sweaters, shawls, granny squares and anything you'll seam look much better for it. Toys, dishcloths and chunky hats often don't need it at all.

What you need

  • Something flat to pin into: interlocking foam blocking mats (often sold as 12 in tiles), a spare bed, or folded towels on a carpet.
  • Rust-proof pins: T-pins or blocking pins with combs. Ordinary pins can rust and mark the yarn.
  • Blocking wires for lace and long straight edges (optional, but they save a lot of pins).
  • A tape measure, the pattern's measurements, a basin and a gentle wool wash (no-rinse kinds are easiest).
  • A steam iron or steamer if you're steam blocking.

How to wet block, step by step

  1. Soak: fill a basin with lukewarm water and a little wool wash. Push the piece under and leave it 15 to 30 minutes so the water gets right into the fibres. Don't agitate wool: rubbing plus warm water felts it.
  2. Squeeze out: lift it out supported in both hands (wet knitting stretches under its own weight), press out the water, then roll it up in a towel and press or stand on it.
  3. Shape: lay it on the mats and pat it into shape, measuring as you go. Pin the corners and centres first, then fill in, every 1 to 2 in on edges (closer for lace points).
  4. Dry: leave it until it's completely dry, usually 24 to 48 hours. A fan speeds it up. Unpin too early and it springs back.

How to steam block

Pin the piece out dry to its measurements, then hold a steam iron or a garment steamer about 1 in above it and let the steam soak in. Don't press down or touch the yarn, especially acrylic, which melts and goes flat and shiny. Pat it into shape with your hand if needed (careful, it's hot) and leave it pinned until it's cool and dry, about half an hour. Steam is quick and suits acrylic, cotton and wool; skip it on mohair halos and textured stitches you don't want flattened, like cables and bobbles, unless you hold the steam well above.

How to spray block

Pin the piece out dry, then mist it with a spray bottle until it's evenly damp, and leave it to dry. It's the gentlest method and the one to use for granny squares, a quick tidy-up or delicate yarn you don't want to soak.

How to block acrylic yarn

Acrylic is plastic, so water alone doesn't reset it: wet blocking only tidies it until the next wash. Heat does reset it. Steam blocking acrylic softens it and makes it drape, and the change is permanent, which is why it's called killing acrylic. A little steam is enough for most projects; a lot makes lace drapey and flat. Always keep the iron off the yarn and try it on your swatch first.

Blocking superwash wool

Superwash wool is treated so it won't felt, but it can grow a lot when wet. Soak it, squeeze it out well, then lay it flat and push it back to size rather than stretching it. Never hang it to dry. If your swatch grew in blocking, measure gauge on the blocked swatch, not the unblocked one.

How to block granny squares

Draw or print a square of the right size on a mat (or use a grid mat), pin each square to it at the corners and middles, spray, and leave to dry. Blocking a batch to the same size makes joining much easier: see how to join granny squares. A blanket can also be blocked after joining, in sections on a bed.

Blocking a gauge swatch

Always block your swatch the same way you'll block the finished piece, then measure. Many yarns change size when blocked, and a swatch measured before blocking can give you a sweater a size too big or small. Then turn your gauge into stitches with the gauge converter.

Before you block, finish the ends: see how to weave in ends (trim the tails after blocking, so they don't pop out). Knitting a sweater? Get the size right first with the sweater size calculator.

Questions crafters ask

How do you block knitting?

Soak it in lukewarm water with wool wash for 15 to 30 minutes, press the water out in a towel, pin it flat to the pattern measurements and leave it until completely dry.

How do you block crochet?

The same way as knitting: wet block wool and cotton, steam block acrylic, or pin it out dry and spray it with water for a quick tidy-up.

Can you block acrylic yarn?

Yes, with steam. Pin it dry and hold a steam iron just above it without touching. The heat sets the new shape permanently, so test on a swatch first.

How long does blocking take to dry?

Wet blocking usually takes 24 to 48 hours, depending on the yarn and the room. Spray blocking dries in a few hours and steam blocking in about half an hour.

Do I need blocking mats?

No. A spare bed, a carpet covered with towels, or an ironing board for small pieces all work. Mats just make pinning and measuring easier.

Should you block before or after seaming?

Before, for sweaters: blocked pieces are flat and easier to sew. A quick block after seaming then evens out the seams.

Do you have to block amigurumi?

No, toys usually aren't blocked. A little steam can smooth a lumpy shape or flatten ears, held well above the toy.
