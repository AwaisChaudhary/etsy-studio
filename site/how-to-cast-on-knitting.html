How to cast on knitting

Casting on puts the first stitches on your needle. Here's the long-tail cast on in four drawings, how long a tail to leave for any number of stitches, and which cast on to use for scarves, hats, socks and firm edges.

Quick answer: the long-tail cast on is the one most knitters use. Pull out a tail of about 1 in (2.5 cm) per stitch in worsted yarn, plus 6 in to weave in (so about 1.3 yd for 40 stitches), make a slip knot and hold the tail over your thumb and the ball yarn over your finger. Go up through the thumb loop, catch the finger strand, come back down and snug it. Repeat. If guessing the tail worries you, use the knitted cast on instead: it needs no tail.

Cast on chooser and tail calculator

I measured my own yarn (most accurate)

Wrap the yarn round your needle 10 times, unwind it and measure that length (Elizabeth Smith's wrap method). Each wrap is about one stitch.

How much tail do I need for a long-tail cast on?

A common rule (Marly Bird's) is about 1 in of tail per stitch, plus about 6 in to weave in: 100 stitches need roughly 106 in, or 3 yd. That fits worsted yarn. Thin yarn makes smaller stitches and needs less; thick yarn needs more, so we scale it by the yarn's typical gauge (our estimate). The most accurate way is to wrap the yarn round your needle once per stitch (or 10 times and multiply), as Elizabeth Smith Knits and Marly Bird both suggest. When in doubt, go longer: a spare foot of yarn costs nothing.

What if I run out of tail?

  • Pull it out and start again with a longer tail. It's annoying but it's only one row.
  • Use two strands instead of a tail. Take one end from the outside of the ball and one from the middle (or use two balls), tie them in a slip knot and cast on with one over your thumb and one over your finger. You never run short. Cut the thumb strand afterwards; the slip knot doesn't count as a stitch here, so drop it at the end of the first row. It leaves two extra ends to weave in.
  • Finish with the knitted cast on. If you're only a few stitches short, Marly Bird suggests adding the last ones with the knitted cast on.

Which cast on is best for beginners?

Teachers disagree. The backward loop is the easiest to make, but the first row is fiddly and loose, so it's better for adding a few stitches. The knitted cast on and the cable cast on use the same move as a knit stitch, so many teachers start with one of them, and there's no tail to guess. The long-tail takes a little longer to learn but it's the one you'll use most, so it's worth learning early.

Which cast on is stretchy?

For anything that has to go over a head, hand or heel, use a stretchy cast on: the long-tail is fairly stretchy, the German twisted (a long-tail with an extra twist) is very stretchy, and the tubular cast on is the most elastic for 1×1 rib. The cable cast on is firm, so keep it for bags, dishcloths and edges you'll pick up stitches from.

Why is my cast on too tight?

  • Use a bigger needle for the cast on only: 1 or 2 sizes up, then switch back for the first row.
  • Cast on over two needles held together, then slide one out. Marly Bird recommends it for ribbing.
  • Leave a gap of about the width of a stitch between stitches on the needle, and don't yank the yarn after each one. They should slide easily.

Does the cast on count as a row?

Usually not: patterns start counting at the first row you knit. But the long-tail cast on looks like a row of knit stitches on one side, so some knitters treat the next row as a wrong-side row (Little Red Window). Follow your pattern if it says which side faces you.

How do I cast on to knit in the round?

  1. Cast on all the stitches onto a circular needle shorter than the piece is around (a 16 in circular for a hat).
  2. Spread them round the needle and check the bottom edge runs along the inside of the circle all the way round, with no twist. A twist can't be fixed later.
  3. Put a marker on the right needle, then knit the first stitch on the left needle with the working yarn, pulling it snug. That joins the round.

Questions crafters ask

How do you cast on in knitting?

The long-tail cast on is the most used: leave a tail of about 1 in per stitch, make a slip knot on the needle, hold the tail over your thumb and the ball yarn over your index finger, go up through the thumb loop, catch the finger strand, come back down and snug the stitch. Repeat.

How long should the tail be for a long-tail cast on?

About 1 in (2.5 cm) per stitch in worsted yarn, plus about 6 in to weave in. That's roughly 1.3 yd for 40 stitches and 2.9 yd for 100. Thin yarn needs less and thick yarn more. Wrapping the yarn round the needle once per stitch gives an exact measure.

What's the easiest cast on for beginners?

The backward loop is the easiest to make, but its first row is fiddly. Many teachers start beginners on the knitted or cable cast on, which use the same move as a knit stitch and need no tail.

What is the stretchiest cast on?

The tubular cast on (for 1×1 rib) and the German twisted cast on are the stretchiest. The long-tail is fairly stretchy too. The cable cast on is firm.

Why is my cast on so tight?

Cast on with a needle 1 or 2 sizes larger, or over two needles held together, and leave a small gap between stitches. Switch back to the right needle for the first row.

Does the cast on count as the first row?

Usually not. Patterns start counting at the first row you knit after the cast on, unless they say otherwise.
