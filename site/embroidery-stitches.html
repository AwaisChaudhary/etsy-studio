Embroidery stitches for beginners

Twelve hand embroidery stitches, each with our own step diagram, what it's for and how many strands to use. Pick what you want to stitch to see the stitches that suit it.

Quick answer: start with running stitch, backstitch, stem stitch, satin stitch, lazy daisy and French knots. Backstitch and stem stitch do the lines and curves, satin stitch fills shapes, lazy daisy makes petals and French knots make dots, so these six cover most beginner patterns. Use 2 or 3 of the 6 strands of embroidery floss and a hoop to keep the fabric tight.

Running stitch

Dashed lines, simple outlines, quilting-style borders and gathering. Strands: 2–3.

  1. Bring the needle up at the start of the line (1).
  2. Go down a stitch length along the line (2).
  3. Come up again a gap further on: the gap is usually the same as, or a little shorter than, the stitch.
  4. Repeat. Keep stitches and gaps even; it helps to load two or three stitches on the needle at once.

Backstitch

Solid, crisp lines: lettering, outlines and fine details. The go-to outline stitch. Strands: 1–2.

  1. Come up one stitch length along the line from the start (1).
  2. Go back down at the very start of the line (2).
  3. Come up one stitch length ahead of your first stitch (3).
  4. Go back down into the end of the previous stitch, sharing its hole. Repeat.

Stem stitch

Smooth curves and flower stems. It looks like a twisted rope. Strands: 2–3.

  1. Come up at the start of the line.
  2. Go down one stitch length along the line, keeping the working thread below the line.
  3. Come up halfway back along that stitch, just beside it.
  4. Go down a full stitch length ahead. Keep the thread on the same side for every stitch.

Split stitch

Bold lines and soft, filled shapes worked in rows. Great on curves. Strands: 2 (even number).

  1. Make one straight stitch along the line.
  2. Come up through the middle of that stitch, splitting its strands.
  3. Go down a stitch length ahead.
  4. Come up through the middle of the new stitch. Repeat.

Satin stitch

Smooth, solid fills for petals, leaves, hearts and small letters. Strands: 2–3.

  1. Outline the shape with split or backstitch first: it gives a neat raised edge.
  2. Come up on one edge of the shape (1) and go down straight across on the opposite edge (2).
  3. Come up right next to where you first came up, and lay the next stitch beside the last.
  4. Keep stitches parallel and close, with no fabric showing. Start in the middle of the shape to keep the angle steady.

Chain stitch

Thick, textured lines, borders and lettering. Fills too, in rows. Strands: 2–4.

  1. Come up at the start of the line.
  2. Go back down into the same hole, leaving a loop on top.
  3. Come up a stitch length along the line, inside the loop, and pull until the loop sits snug.
  4. Go down inside the loop at the same hole you came up through to start the next link. End with a tiny stitch over the last loop.

Lazy daisy (detached chain)

Petals and little leaves. Five or six make a quick flower. Strands: 2–3.

  1. Come up at the inner end of the petal.
  2. Go back down in the same hole, leaving a loop.
  3. Come up at the tip of the petal, inside the loop.
  4. Go down just outside the loop to hold it with a tiny stitch. Repeat round the centre.

French knot

Dots, flower centres, eyes and textured fills. Strands: 2–3.

  1. Come up where you want the knot.
  2. Hold the thread taut and wrap it twice round the needle.
  3. Keep holding the thread and put the needle back down just next to where it came up (not in the same hole).
  4. Pull the needle through slowly while still holding the thread snug, so the knot forms on the surface.

Blanket stitch

Edges of felt and fabric, appliqué, and decorative borders. Strands: 2–6.

  1. Come up on the edge line (or at the fabric edge).
  2. Go down above it at the stitch height and a stitch width along.
  3. Come up on the edge line directly below, with the thread under the needle.
  4. Pull snug so the loop catches along the edge. Repeat, keeping heights and spacing even.

Fly stitch

Leaves, ferns, grass and borders. Stack them for a fern. Strands: 2–3.

  1. Come up at the top left of the V (1).
  2. Go down at the top right (2), but don't pull tight.
  3. Come up at the point of the V, below and between, inside the loop (3).
  4. Go down a little below to hold the V with a small straight stitch (4).

Cross stitch

Counted designs on Aida or evenweave; samplers and pixel-style pictures. Strands: 2 on 14–count.

  1. Work bottom-left to top-right half stitches along the row.
  2. Come back along the row, top-left to bottom-right, to finish each X.
  3. Keep every top leg going the same way so the work looks even.
  4. Use our cross stitch calculator for the fabric size and floss.

Seed stitch

Speckled, textured fills and soft shading. Strands: 1–2.

  1. Make a tiny straight stitch.
  2. Come up a short distance away and make another at a different angle.
  3. Scatter them evenly; for denser shading, work them closer together.
  4. Work pairs side by side for a bolder seed.

In the diagrams, green numbers are where the needle comes up, navy numbers where it goes down, the dotted line is the thread at the back, and the pale stitch is the one being made.

What you need to start

  • Fabric: a tightly woven cotton or linen. Calico or quilting cotton is cheap and fine for practice.
  • A hoop a little bigger than your design. 6 in (15 cm) is an easy first size.
  • Embroidery floss: six strands twisted together. Cut about 18 in (45 cm) at a time and separate the strands you need.
  • An embroidery (crewel) needle, size 5 to 9: it has a long eye for the floss.
  • Small sharp scissors and a water-soluble pen or pencil to mark the design.

How to start and finish without a knot

Knots are fine for practice, but they can show as lumps and pull through loose weaves. For an even number of strands, use the loop start: take one strand, fold it in half, thread the two cut ends, come up from the back, go down for your first stitch and pass the needle through the loop at the back before you pull. For any count, a waste knot works too: knot the end and go in from the front a few inches away along the line you'll stitch, so your stitches cover the tail at the back; snip the knot off later. To finish, run the needle under three or four stitches at the back and trim.

How many strands should I use?

Most patterns use 2 or 3 strands for lines and 2 to 4 for fills. One strand gives fine detail like whiskers; six gives bold lines and quick fills. Split stitch works best with an even number, so it splits neatly. On 14-count Aida, cross stitch is usually worked with 2 strands.

Which stitch should I learn first?

Backstitch. It's the outline stitch most patterns use, it's easy to keep neat, and it's also the base for other stitches. Then add stem stitch for curves, satin stitch for fills, and lazy daisy and French knots for flowers. Stitch each one in a row on scrap fabric as a sampler before you start a pattern.

Why does my satin stitch look messy?

  • Outline first. A row of split stitch round the edge gives the satin stitches something to sit over.
  • Keep them short. Stitches longer than about 1 cm (⅜ in) snag and sag; split a big shape into sections.
  • Keep the fabric drum tight in the hoop and don't pull the thread tight, which puckers the fabric.

Questions crafters ask

What are the basic embroidery stitches for beginners?

Running stitch, backstitch, stem stitch, satin stitch, lazy daisy (detached chain) and French knots. Add split stitch, chain stitch, blanket stitch, fly stitch and seed stitch as you go.

What is the easiest embroidery stitch?

Running stitch: up, down, leave a gap, repeat. Backstitch is next, and it's the one most patterns use for outlines.

What is the difference between backstitch and stem stitch?

Backstitch makes a flat, crisp line of separate stitches sharing holes, best for letters. Stem stitch overlaps each stitch with the last, making a rope-like line that follows curves smoothly.

How many strands of embroidery floss should I use?

Usually 2 or 3 of the 6 strands for lines and 2 to 4 for fills. Use 1 for fine detail and 6 for bold lines.

How do I start embroidery without a knot?

Use the loop start for an even number of strands: fold one strand in half, thread the cut ends, make your first stitch and pass the needle through the loop at the back. Or use a waste knot placed on the front where later stitches will cover the tail.

Why does my French knot pull through the fabric?

The needle went back down in the same hole it came up from. Go down just beside it, and keep the wraps snug while you pull through.
