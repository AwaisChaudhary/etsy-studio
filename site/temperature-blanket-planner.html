Temperature blanket planner: colours, ranges and yarn

Set your coldest and hottest temperatures, how many degrees each colour covers, your stitch and yarn. Get a colour key you can edit, how long the blanket will be, the foundation chain or cast-on, and how much yarn to buy for each colour. Paste last year's temperatures to see the real blanket.

Quick answer: a temperature blanket has one row (or two) for every day of the year, coloured by that day's temperature. Most makers use 8 to 10 colours, each covering about 5 to 10°F (or 3 to 5°C), from their area's usual coldest day to its hottest. 365 rows of single crochet in worsted yarn come out about 102 in long, and a 50 in wide blanket needs roughly 4,300 yards. Garter stitch at two rows a day is shorter, about 81 in. These are estimates at a typical gauge: put your own swatch in below.

How many colours do you need for a temperature blanket?

Most temperature blankets use between 6 and 12 colours, and 8 to 10 is the most common. Fewer colours give bold, wide stripes; more colours show small changes from day to day but cost more yarn and more ends to weave in. Work it out from your weather: take the range from your usual coldest day to your usual hottest, and divide it by how many degrees you want each colour to cover.

Each row counts the end colours too: one for anything colder than your lowest number and one for anything hotter than your highest. Many makers keep those two for rare days and pick a bold contrast for them, so a record heatwave or a deep freeze stands out.

How do I choose my temperature ranges?

  1. Look up last year (or the last few years) for your town. Weather Underground's history pages, NOAA's Climate Data Online (US), the Met Office (UK) and your own national weather service all list daily highs and lows.
  2. Note the usual lowest and highest daily values for the temperature you'll track. Ignore one freak day: it can go in an end colour.
  3. Pick a step. A spread of about 100°F suits 10°F per colour; a narrower spread of 60 to 70°F suits 5 to 7°F, and in Celsius 2 to 5° per colour is usual.
  4. Check the middle. Most days fall in the middle bands, so give those colours you love, and buy more of them.
Tip: paste last year's daily highs into the planner. It counts the days for each colour, so you can see if one colour would take over half the blanket and change the step before you buy yarn.

Should I use the high, the low or the average?

Any of them works; just pick one and stick with it all year. The daily high is the most common choice because it's the number in every forecast. The average gives gentler stripes. For more detail, work two rows a day: the high, then the low. That doubles the rows, so use a thinner yarn or a narrower blanket, or the blanket gets very long.

Why is my temperature blanket so long?

Because 365 rows is a lot of rows. Even short single crochet stitches in worsted yarn add up to well over 2 metres, which is longer than a queen bed. To keep it a usable size, choose a shorter stitch or thinner yarn (DK single crochet comes out about 88 in), or knit garter stitch, where two rows make one ridge. Moss stitch, a mix of single crochet and chains, is popular because it lies flat and shows colour stripes neatly (about 93 in in worsted). Some makers work each day as a granny square or hexagon instead, then join them in a grid, which gives a square blanket with no length problem.

How much yarn does a temperature blanket take?

A full-size year blanket usually takes 3,000 to 5,000 yards of worsted yarn, split across your colours. The planner gives the total and an even share per colour, or the exact share if you paste real temperatures. For your own yarn, crochet one row, weigh the ball before and after, and multiply by the number of days: 7 g a day for 365 days is 2,555 g, plus about 10 percent spare.

Buying it all at once keeps dye lots matching, while buying a ball of each colour and adding more as the months go spreads the cost. If you buy as you go, choose a yarn that's widely stocked so you can still get it in October. Machine-washable acrylic or a cotton blend is easy to care for in a blanket this big.

Crocheting it in rows? Change colour on the last stitch of the row and carry colours up the side when a stripe is 2 or 4 rows: our guide to changing colours in crochet has a stripe planner that shows which colours to carry and which to cut.

How do I keep up with a temperature blanket?

  • Log temperatures daily, even when you don't crochet. Then you can catch up in batches at the weekend.
  • Add a month row in a neutral or border colour between months. It makes the blanket easy to read and gives you a checkpoint.
  • Count rows with our row counter, one tap per day.
  • Weave ends in as you go, or leave 5 in tails at the edges and knot them into a fringe at the end.
  • Started late? Use the past months' temperatures to catch up, or make it a "last year" blanket from a full year of history.

Questions crafters ask

How many colours do you need for a temperature blanket?

Most use 8 to 10 colours, each covering about 5 to 10°F (3 to 5°C), plus one colour for anything colder than your range and one for anything hotter. Fewer colours give bolder stripes.

What temperature ranges should I use?

Look up last year's daily highs (or lows) for your town, find the usual coldest and hottest, and split that spread into equal steps. About 10°F per colour suits a 100°F spread; 5 to 7°F suits a narrower one.

How big is a temperature blanket?

One row a day for a year is 365 rows. In worsted single crochet that's roughly 102 in long at a typical gauge, and in moss stitch about 93 in, so many makers use DK yarn, garter stitch or a granny square per day to keep it a usable size.

How much yarn do I need for a temperature blanket?

A 50 in wide worsted blanket in single crochet takes about 4,300 yards (our estimate). Weigh one row of your own yarn and multiply by 365, then add 10 percent.

Should I use the high or the average temperature?

Either works. The daily high is the most common because it's easy to find. You can also work two rows a day, the high and then the low, but that doubles the length.

What if I miss a day or fall behind?

Keep writing the temperatures down every day, then catch up when you have time. Weather history sites keep the daily records, so you can always look up a missed day.
