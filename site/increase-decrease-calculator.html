Increase and decrease evenly calculator

Type how many stitches you have and how many to add or take away. You get a written line you can follow, with the increases or decreases spread as evenly as the maths allows.

Add or take away?

You'll end with 72 stitches.

Working
Quick answer: divide the stitches you have by the number of increases or decreases. For 60 stitches and 12 increases, 60 ÷ 12 = 5, so work K5, M1 twelve times in the round, or start with K2 or K3 when working flat so the first and last increase sit away from the edges. If it doesn't divide exactly, alternate the shorter and longer gaps.

How do you increase evenly across a row?

  1. Divide. Stitches now ÷ increases. 80 ÷ 10 = 8, so there's an increase every 8 stitches.
  2. Working flat, split one gap. Put half a gap at each end: K4, M1, then (K8, M1) nine times, K4. That keeps increases off the selvedge, where they'd be hidden in the seam or look untidy.
  3. In the round, there are no edges. (K8, M1) ten times brings you back to the start.
  4. If it doesn't divide exactly, some gaps are one stitch longer. 62 ÷ 8 = 7.75, so six gaps of 8 and two of 7. The calculator spreads the longer gaps out instead of bunching them at one end.

How do you decrease evenly?

The same way, with one difference: each decrease uses two stitches. If you need to decrease 10 from 90, every 9 stitches hold one decrease, so you knit 7 and then knit 2 together. Forgetting this is the most common reason a row ends with the wrong count. In crochet a decrease (sc2tog) also works two stitches together: our guide to how to decrease in crochet shows sc2tog and the invisible decrease step by step.

Lean directions as described in standard knitting references. When a pattern just says "inc" or "dec", it usually means the simplest one: kfb or M1 for knitting, 2 sc in one stitch or sc2tog for crochet.

Increases worked into a stitch use it up

A make one (M1) is lifted from the bar between stitches, so it doesn't use a stitch. Kfb and the crochet increase (2 sc in the same stitch) are worked into a stitch, so that stitch counts as part of the gap. "Sc 4, inc" is 5 stitches of the old round becoming 6. Pick the right type in the calculator and it adjusts.

Crochet circles and hat crowns: if every round starts with its increase in the same place, the circle turns into a hexagon. Tick "Stagger" on alternate rounds to start halfway into a repeat. The count is the same; only the position moves.

Questions crafters ask

How do I increase evenly across a row in knitting?

Divide your stitches by the number of increases to get the gap. Working flat, split one gap between the two ends: for 80 stitches and 10 increases, K4, M1, (K8, M1) nine times, K4. In the round, just repeat (K8, M1) ten times.

How do I decrease evenly across a row?

Divide your stitches by the number of decreases, then knit two fewer than that before each k2tog, because a decrease uses two stitches. For 90 stitches and 10 decreases: (K7, k2tog) ten times gives 80.

What if the stitches don't divide evenly by the increases?

Some gaps are one stitch longer than others. For 62 stitches and 8 increases, six gaps are 8 stitches and two are 7. Spread the longer gaps out; the calculator does this for you.

Does kfb count as a stitch when spacing increases?

Yes. Kfb and the crochet 2 sc in one stitch are worked into a stitch, so that stitch is part of the gap. M1 is made between stitches and uses none.

Why does my crochet circle look like a hexagon?

The increases are stacking in the same place every round. Start alternate rounds halfway into the repeat, so the increases fall between the ones below.

What's the difference between k2tog and ssk?

Both knit 2 stitches together to take one away. K2tog leans to the right and ssk leans to the left, so patterns often pair them on either side of a V-neck or raglan line.

How do I decrease 6 stitches evenly in a crochet round of 36?

6 decreases use 12 stitches, leaving 24 plain stitches, 4 before each decrease: (sc 4, sc2tog) 6 times gives 30.
